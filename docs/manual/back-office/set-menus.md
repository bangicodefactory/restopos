# Set menus

A set menu — a formule, a prix fixe, a lunch deal — is a product the customer buys once and then
chooses their way through. This screen is where the choosing is defined.

The vocabulary matters, because the screen is organised around it:

- A **menu** is a product, like anything else you sell. It carries the price.
- A **course** is one of the choices the menu offers: *Starters*, *Mains*, *Dessert or coffee*. This
  screen lists courses, not menus.
- A course offers **dishes** — the actual items the customer picks between.

One course can belong to several menus. A *Dessert* course built once can be offered on the lunch
menu and the evening menu both.

## Building one

Work in this order.

**1. Make the courses.** *New course*, one per part of the meal. Give each one a name your staff
would recognise on the register.

**2. Add the dishes.** Open a course and add the items it offers. Each dish can carry a
**supplement** — the fillet is three euros more than the chicken. A negative supplement is allowed
and is a real thing on a menu: the fish option two euros cheaper.

**3. Attach the courses to the menu.** This is the step that matters and the one that is easy to
miss. **Until a course is attached to a menu, the register never asks the customer to choose
anything** — the menu is rung up as an ordinary product at its own price and the kitchen receives a
ticket for a set menu with no dishes on it.

A course that belongs to no menu is flagged on both screens, because there is no other way to notice.

## Choices included, and the maximum

**Choices included** is how many picks the menu price already covers. **Maximum choices** is how many
the register will accept at all.

One included and a maximum of two means the customer may take a second dish from that course, and
they are charged the course's weight for it. Included cannot exceed the maximum — that would give
away picks the register will not let anyone make.

## Weight in the menu

This is the number people misread, so it is worth being precise.

**It is not what the customer pays.** The menu's own price is what the customer pays. The weight
decides how that price is *divided across the courses* on the receipt and on the kitchen ticket.

A 30 € menu with starters weighted 10 and mains weighted 20 prints as 10 € of starter and 20 € of
main — whatever those dishes cost on their own. That is what makes a set menu a set menu: a 24 €
steak inside a 30 € menu still only carries its course's share.

Two consequences:

- A course weighted at zero carries none of the menu price. The other courses carry all of it.
- When the split does not divide evenly, the remainder lands on the last course. A 9,99 € menu across
  two equal courses prints 5,00 € and 4,99 € — the half-cent is never thrown away.

The **Price alone** column on the dish list shows what each item sells for by itself. It is there for
context when you are deciding the weights; it plays no part in the split.

## Removing a course

A course cannot be removed while a menu still offers it — remove it from those menus first,
otherwise the menu quietly stops asking for that part of the meal.

It also cannot be removed once it has been sold: order lines record which course each choice came
from, and that is how a past receipt explains what the customer actually had. Switch it off instead.
